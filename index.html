About Hummingbirds

Hummingbirds are among the most fascinating creatures on the planet. Belonging to the family Trochilidae, these tiny birds are native to the Americas and are renowned for their remarkable ability to hover in mid-air by rapidly flapping their wings up to 80 times per second.

With iridescent plumage that shifts color in the light and a metabolism so fast their hearts can beat over 1,200 times per minute, hummingbirds are true engineering marvels of the natural world. They are the only birds that can fly backwards and even upside down.

There are over 360 recognized species of hummingbirds, ranging from the tiny Bee Hummingbird — the smallest bird in the world at just 5 cm — to the Giant Hummingbird, which can reach up to 23 cm in length.

Notable Species

💎

Ruby-throated Hummingbird

Archilochus colubris

The most common hummingbird in eastern North America. Males display a brilliant ruby-red throat patch (gorget) that gleams in sunlight. They migrate across the Gulf of Mexico — a non-stop flight of over 800 km.

✨

Bee Hummingbird

Mellisuga helenae

Native to Cuba, this is the smallest living bird in the world, measuring just 5–6 cm and weighing under 2 grams. Despite its minuscule size, it beats its wings up to 200 times per second during courtship displays.

👑

Anna's Hummingbird

Calypte anna

A year-round resident of the western United States, Anna's Hummingbird is known for its spectacular dive display. Males plunge from heights of 40 meters, reaching speeds that generate forces of nearly 10G.

☄

Sword-billed Hummingbird

Ensifera ensifera

The only bird with a bill longer than its body (excluding the tail). Found in the high Andes of South America, this species co-evolved with long, tubular flowers that no other pollinator can reach.

🌈

Fiery-throated Hummingbird

Panterpe insignis

Found in the cloud forests of Costa Rica and Panama, this species displays an extraordinary rainbow of colors on its throat — from blue and green to orange and red — all shifting with the angle of light.

⛅

Giant Hummingbird

Patagona gigas

At 23 cm long and weighing 18–24 grams, this is the largest hummingbird species. Found along the Andes from Ecuador to Chile, it flaps its wings at a relatively slow 10–15 beats per second.

Fascinating Facts

01

Torpor: A Nightly Near-Death

To survive cold nights, hummingbirds enter a state called torpor — a deep, hibernation-like sleep where their metabolic rate drops by up to 95% and body temperature can fall to as low as 12°C (54°F). Their heart rate slows from over 1,000 to as few as 50 beats per minute.

02

Memory Champions

Hummingbirds have an exceptional memory for a bird their size. They can remember every flower they've visited and how long it takes each flower to refill with nectar. Their hippocampus — the brain region responsible for spatial memory — is up to five times larger (relative to body size) than in other birds.

03

Insatiable Appetite

A hummingbird must consume roughly half its body weight in sugar each day. They visit between 1,000 and 2,000 flowers daily and supplement their diet with small insects and spiders for protein. If humans had the same metabolic rate, we would need to eat approximately 155,000 calories per day.

04

Feather Physics

Hummingbird feathers aren't actually colored by pigment — their iridescent appearance comes from the microscopic structure of their feathers, which refract light like a prism. The same feather can appear black, green, red, or gold depending on the angle of observation.

05

Ancient Lineage

The oldest known hummingbird fossils are approximately 30 million years old and were found in Europe — not the Americas. This suggests hummingbirds originated in the Old World and later migrated to and diversified across the Americas, eventually going extinct in Europe.

Habitat & Range

Tropical Rainforests

The greatest diversity of hummingbird species is found in the tropical rainforests of Central and South America, particularly in Colombia and Ecuador, which together are home to over 130 species. The dense canopy and year-round flower availability make these forests an ideal habitat.

Cloud Forests

High-altitude cloud forests in the Andes support numerous endemic species like the Sword-billed Hummingbird and the Sapphire-vented Puffleg. These misty habitats at elevations of 1,500–3,500 meters offer unique flowering plants found nowhere else on Earth.

Deserts & Scrublands

Species like Costa's Hummingbird thrive in the arid deserts of the southwestern United States and Mexico. These hardy birds have adapted to extreme heat and limited water by timing their breeding to coincide with the blooming of desert wildflowers and cacti.

Temperate Woodlands

Several species, including Anna's and Rufous Hummingbirds, inhabit temperate forests along the Pacific coast of North America. The Rufous Hummingbird makes one of the longest migratory journeys relative to body size of any bird — up to 6,000 km from Alaska to Mexico.

Conservation

While many hummingbird species remain abundant, at least 28 species are currently listed as vulnerable, endangered, or critically endangered by the IUCN. The primary threats include habitat loss from deforestation, climate change altering flowering patterns, and pesticide use reducing insect prey populations.

Conservation efforts focus on protecting critical habitat corridors, especially along migration routes. Planting native flowering plants, avoiding pesticides in gardens, and supporting protected areas in tropical regions all contribute to hummingbird conservation.

How You Can Help

  • Plant native, nectar-rich flowers in your garden
  • Avoid using pesticides and herbicides near flowering plants
  • Provide clean hummingbird feeders with plain sugar water (4:1 water to sugar ratio — never use dyes)
  • Support conservation organizations protecting tropical habitats
  • Keep cats indoors to reduce predation on wild birds
  • Participate in community science projects like eBird to help track populations
